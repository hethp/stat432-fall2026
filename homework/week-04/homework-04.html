At λ = 0.12, X3 (coefficient 0.30) is selected in 95.0% of repetitions, X4 (coefficient 0.15) in 61.5%, each member of the correlated pair in roughly half, and the noise covariates in about 18.6% on average. The two marginal selection frequencies are similar because the construction treats the two covariates symmetrically: they have the same population coefficient (0.15), the same marginal distribution, and an exchangeable joint distribution, so neither is systematically preferred. The remaining gap is consistent with Monte Carlo variation.

As λ decreases, “neither” disappears (from about 30.5% at λ = 0.24 to 0.5% at λ = 0.08) and single-member fits dominate; “both” remains uncommon at every penalty (at most 3%). Once one member represents the shared direction, the partial residual seen by the other member carries little remaining signal, so coordinate descent keeps one sparse representative rather than splitting the effect. This is why the lasso can produce a sparse fitted rule that omits one member of the pair even though both coefficients are nonzero in the generating equation: the omitted member’s information is carried almost perfectly by its substitute.

The two independent signals separate cleanly by strength. At λ = 0.12, X3 (coefficient 0.30) is selected about 95% of the time, while X4 (coefficient 0.15) is selected about 61.5%; the noise covariates average about 18.6% and fall to about 1% at λ = 0.24. For a covariate independent of the others, the score is centered near its population coefficient with spread about 1/√n, so the weaker coefficient 0.15 leaves its score inside the threshold interval [−λ, λ] more often than the stronger coefficient 0.30.

The correlated pair supplies two interpretation cautions. First, a fitted zero does not establish a zero population coefficient: each member of the pair has a true coefficient of 0.15, yet each is omitted in about half of the repetitions at λ = 0.12. Second, a nonzero fitted coefficient does not establish that the variable is uniquely important: the selected member of the pair is standing in for information shared with a covariate that the same fitted rule may omit. Selection in one sample describes one sparse predictive rule, not the structure of the generating equation.

“Both” now dominates the pair outcomes at every penalty: 93.5% to 96.0% across the grid, compared with only 0.5% to 3.0% under the lasso. Single-member outcomes nearly vanish, and “neither” falls from 2.5% at the largest penalty to 0.5% at the smallest. The two covariates are therefore properly selected together in the large majority of repetitions.

The squared ℓ2 part of the penalty explains the change. The two covariates are nearly identical, so splitting their shared effect leaves the fitted values and the ℓ1 cost almost unchanged no matter how the sum is divided. The squared ℓ2 part is not indifferent: at a fixed sum, β̂12 + β̂22 is smallest when the effect is shared equally. The strictly convex part of the penalty therefore removes the indifference between the two members, and the fitted rule keeps both in the large majority of repetitions.

Part (c)

The noise covariates are still screened increasingly as the penalty grows, but much less aggressively than with the lasso at the same numerical penalty. The noise average falls from about 65% at λ = 0.08 to about 19% at λ = 0.24, whereas the lasso’s average fell from about 37% to about 1% over the same grid. With an equal mix, a standardized coordinate leaves zero only when the absolute value of its score exceeds λ × 0.5, half the lasso’s threshold at the same λ. The elastic net therefore needs a larger numerical penalty to screen as aggressively as the lasso.

On this grid, λ = 0.24 comes closest to both goals: both members of the pair are selected together in about 93.5% of repetitions, and the noise average is at its grid minimum of about 19–20%. Stronger screening would require a still larger penalty.

Part (b)

The second covariate (true coefficient −2) enters first, followed by the first covariate (true coefficient +1). The third covariate (true coefficient 0) enters last. With independent population covariates, a larger true coefficient in absolute value tends to produce a larger score and entry at a larger penalty. Here the coefficient of size 2 enters before the coefficient of size 1, consistent with this ordering. As the penalty decreases toward zero, both active curves move toward their least-squares values.

Part (c)

The third covariate enters the path at the two smallest penalties (λ = 0.08 and λ = 0.03), reaching a fitted slope of 0.1298 at λ = 0.03. Its least-squares slope is 0.1724. A covariate with a zero population coefficient can enter the path once the penalty is small enough, and whether it does depends on the realized sample. The path is therefore not a ranking of importance.

At the smallest grid value (λ = 0.03), the fit is (0.7963, -2.1807, 0.1298), compared with least squares (0.8276, -2.2191, 0.1724). Each active coefficient sits slightly closer to zero than its least-squares value. As λ approaches zero, the penalty vanishes and the penalized fit approaches the unique least-squares fit for these data.

Question 4: Tuning and comparing penalized regressions for diabetes prediction

Three penalized regressions were tuned on the diabetes training set (n = 353) using ten-fold cross-validation with the supplied fold assignments. The scaler was placed inside a Pipeline so that each fold re-estimates the centers and scales. The penalty grid contained 61 logarithmically spaced values from 10−4 to 102.
